# Executing High-Angle Specialized Access Operations for Aerospace Infrastructure

**Author: Shane Thomas Strough**
**Field Notes Series — [shanestrough.com](https://shanestrough.com)**

---

## The Thesis

There is a difference between working at height and working with height. Working at height means you tolerate the elevation. You clip in, you do your job, you get down. Working with height means you have engineered the access, trained the team, planned the logistics, and built the rescue capability so that the elevation is an operational parameter — not a risk you are surviving.

At Blue Origin's LC-36 Vehicle Access Tower at Cape Canaveral, I led electrical and controls operations at 586 feet and above. Six total mobilizations. Zero incidents. Not zero lost-time incidents — zero incidents. No first aids. No near misses. No equipment drops. No rescue activations.

That record was not luck. It was the product of deliberate planning, relentless training, and a team culture where every person understood that at 586 feet, there is no margin for improvisation. The marine salt air corrodes everything. The wind changes behavior above 400 feet. OEM support does not exist outside business hours. When something goes wrong, you fix it with what you brought, or you do not fix it.

This article covers how we executed high-angle specialized access operations on one of the most demanding aerospace structures in the country — and the principles that apply to any high-angle, high-consequence infrastructure program.

## The Structure: Blue Origin LC-36

Launch Complex 36 at Cape Canaveral Space Force Station has a history that spans the Mercury, Atlas, and Centaur programs. Blue Origin's Vehicle Access Tower at LC-36 is a modern steel structure designed to service the New Glenn orbital launch vehicle. The tower provides access to the vehicle at multiple elevation levels for payload integration, fueling operations, and pre-launch servicing.

The structure rises over 300 feet and operations on associated infrastructure placed my team at working elevations of 586 feet and above. At that height, you are not on a building — you are on a structure that moves. Thermal expansion causes measurable steel growth over the course of a day. Wind loading at the top of the tower is dramatically different from ground level. The Cape Canaveral coastal environment adds salt air, humidity, and the occasional lightning cell that moves in faster than any weather forecast can predict.

The scope of our work included restoring FAA-compliant obstruction lighting systems, communications infrastructure diagnostics, electrical distribution troubleshooting, and specialized access operations for equipment that could only be reached by trained technical climbers. Every component on that tower was subject to the corrosive salt environment. Every intervention required detailed planning because the logistics of getting people, tools, and materials to working elevation on a structure with limited access paths is a problem unto itself.

## FAA Compliance Restoration: AC 70/7460-1M

The Federal Aviation Administration requires that any structure exceeding 200 feet AGL (above ground level) maintain obstruction lighting and marking per Advisory Circular 70/7460-1M. This is not optional. A structure that does not meet FAA obstruction lighting requirements is a hazard to air navigation, and the FAA takes that seriously. Cape Canaveral is adjacent to active military and civilian flight corridors. A non-compliant tower is a safety deficiency and a regulatory violation.

When we mobilized, the obstruction lighting system had deficiencies attributable to the marine environment. Salt corrosion had degraded wiring, connectors, and fixtures. Some light levels had failed entirely. Others were intermittent — functional during the day when the photocell was not commanding them on, but failing to illuminate at night when they were actually needed.

**The diagnostic challenge at 586 feet:** You cannot troubleshoot an obstruction lighting system from the ground. You cannot see whether a fixture is corroded, a connector is degraded, or a conduit seal has failed unless you are physically at the fixture. And the fixtures are distributed across the structure at multiple elevations — not concentrated in one accessible area.

Every diagnostic trip to the top of the tower required:

- A weather window (sustained winds below 25 mph, no lightning within 30 nautical miles)
- A team of minimum four people (two climbers, one ground rescue standby, one operations coordinator)
- Complete high-angle rescue equipment staged and ready
- A detailed work plan identifying every fixture to be inspected, the test procedures for each, and the materials required for any anticipated repairs
- Communication with Cape Canaveral Range Safety for airspace coordination

We could not afford to climb the tower, discover a problem, descend to get a part, and climb again. Every mobilization to elevation had to be self-sufficient. That meant understanding the failure modes before we climbed, pre-positioning every conceivable replacement part, and carrying diagnostic equipment capable of isolating faults in the field.

**The restoration:** We mapped every obstruction lighting fixture on the tower — location, circuit, photocell association, and condition assessment. We developed a prioritized remediation plan that addressed immediate life-safety deficiencies first (non-functional lights on the highest points of the structure), then degraded systems, then preventive maintenance. Each mobilization to elevation addressed a defined set of fixtures per the plan. We tested every circuit end-to-end: from the lighting panel through the conduit run, through every junction box, to the fixture. We replaced corroded connectors, re-sealed conduit penetrations against moisture intrusion, replaced failed drivers and LEDs, and verified photocell operation for automatic dusk-to-dawn operation.

The FAA compliance was restored across all deficient systems. The tower met AC 70/7460-1M requirements for obstruction lighting and marking when we completed our scope. The documentation package included as-built wiring diagrams, test reports for every circuit, and a maintenance schedule tailored to the marine environment that accounted for accelerated corrosion rates.

## High-Angle Technical Rescue Certification

This is the part that separates a high-angle electrical operation from a regular electrical operation: every member of the team was certified in high-angle technical rescue before they climbed the first rung.

Not fall protection training. Not your standard OSHA 10-hour with a section on tie-off points. Technical rescue certification — the ability to perform a rescue of an incapacitated worker from a structure at elevation using rope systems, mechanical advantage, lowering devices, and patient packaging.

**Why this matters:** At 586 feet on a structure with limited access, if a worker becomes incapacitated — heat exhaustion, cardiac event, equipment failure, injury from a falling object — the local fire department cannot get to them. The standard emergency response model of "call 911 and wait for the ladder truck" does not work when the patient is 500+ feet above the ground on a structure that a fire truck ladder cannot reach. The rescue must be performed by the people who are already there, using the equipment they brought with them, within the critical window that determines the outcome.

**The training program:** Every team member completed a multi-day high-angle technical rescue course before the first mobilization. The curriculum included:

- Anchor system construction and evaluation
- Rope systems — lowering, raising, and mechanical advantage
- Pickoff rescue — removing an incapacitated worker from a compromised position on the structure
- Litter (Stokes basket) packaging and vertical/horizontal lowering
- Patient assessment and stabilization at elevation
- Communication protocols specific to high-angle rescue operations
- Equipment inspection — every carabiner, every pulley, every rope is inspected before every use per manufacturer specifications and NFPA 1983 standards

**The equipment:** We maintained a dedicated rescue equipment cache at the job site. Two complete rope rescue systems. Stokes basket. Patient harness. Mechanical advantage hardware. Anchor straps rated for the steel connections on the tower. All of it inspected monthly and before every mobilization. None of it was dual-purpose — the rescue equipment was rescue equipment, never used for work positioning or material handling.

**The rescue plan:** Before every mobilization to elevation, we briefed the rescue plan. Every person on the team knew their role: who initiates the rescue, who manages the rope system, who packages the patient, who coordinates with ground-level medical response. The plan included primary and contingency rescue routes based on the specific work location on the tower. We briefed the plan, walked through the scenarios verbally, and confirmed equipment readiness.

In six mobilizations, we never activated the rescue plan. That is the point. The training, the equipment, the planning — all of it exists so that when the moment arrives, the response is automatic, not improvised. The fact that we never needed it does not mean we wasted the investment. It means we were prepared, and that preparation contributed to the operational discipline that prevented incidents in the first place.

## Marine/Salt Environment Diagnostics

Cape Canaveral is not a friendly environment for electrical systems. The facility sits on a barrier island on the Atlantic coast of Central Florida. Salt-laden air is a constant. Humidity regularly exceeds 80%. Afternoon thunderstorms are a daily occurrence from May through October. UV exposure is relentless.

Every electrical component on the tower was subject to accelerated degradation compared to an inland installation. Corrosion rates on exposed steel, copper, and aluminum connections were significantly higher than standard tables predict for inland environments. Standard wire connectors that would last 20 years in a dry climate were showing corrosion degradation in 18 months.

**Diagnostic approach:** We could not assume that standard troubleshooting methods would identify all failure modes. A megger test on a conduit run might show acceptable insulation resistance at 9 AM when the conduit interior is relatively dry, and fail at 3 PM after the afternoon humidity spike has introduced moisture through a degraded seal. We scheduled our most critical diagnostic tests during the worst environmental conditions — afternoon, high humidity, post-rain — because that is when the system is under the most stress and latent defects reveal themselves.

**Connector diagnostics.** Every bolted connection, every terminal, every splice on the tower was visually inspected and tested. We used thermal imaging to identify hot connections that indicated increased resistance from corrosion. We used contact resistance testing (Ductor testing) on critical connections to quantify the degradation. Any connection with contact resistance above the manufacturer's specified threshold was disassembled, cleaned, re-coated with corrosion inhibitor, and re-torqued.

**Conduit seal integrity.** Every conduit penetration and junction box on the tower was inspected for seal integrity. In a marine environment, conduit is not just a raceway — it is a sealed environmental barrier. A failed conduit seal allows moisture-laden salt air to enter the conduit system, where it condenses on wire insulation and terminal points. Over time, this causes insulation degradation and conductor corrosion from the inside. We replaced every degraded seal with marine-rated silicone sealant and verified seal integrity by pressurization testing on critical runs.

**Material specification for replacements.** Every replacement component we installed was specified for marine/coastal environments. Stainless steel hardware instead of zinc-plated. Marine-rated terminal connectors with sealed insulation. UV-stabilized cable ties instead of standard nylon. Fiberglass junction boxes instead of steel where the installation allowed it. The upfront material cost was higher. The lifecycle cost was dramatically lower because the replacements would actually survive the environment.

## MTTR Reduction Through Field Troubleshooting Companions

Mean Time to Repair is the critical metric for any maintenance or commissioning operation, but it becomes existentially important when the work location requires hours of preparation to access. At ground level, a high MTTR means the system is down longer than necessary. At 586 feet, a high MTTR means the team is at elevation longer than necessary — extending the exposure window, burning weather windows, and fatiguing the climbing team.

I introduced what I call "field troubleshooting companions" — pre-built, pre-tested diagnostic and repair kits tailored to the specific systems on the tower. Each companion was a self-contained package that included:

- Diagnostic equipment configured for the specific system (multimeter with the correct test leads, megger with the correct voltage setting, thermal imager with the correct emissivity profile)
- Replacement components for the most likely failure modes, pre-labeled and pre-cut to length where applicable
- Wiring diagrams specific to the work location, laminated for weather resistance
- A step-by-step troubleshooting decision tree that guided the technician from symptom to root cause to repair without needing to consult an engineer on the ground

**The impact:** Before the troubleshooting companions, a typical diagnostic trip to elevation went like this: climb the tower (45 minutes). Identify the problem (30 minutes). Discover you need a part or tool that is on the ground (5 seconds of frustration). Descend (30 minutes). Retrieve the part (15 minutes). Climb again (45 minutes). Complete the repair (30 minutes). Descend (30 minutes). Total: approximately 3.5 hours for a 30-minute repair.

After the troubleshooting companions: climb the tower (45 minutes). Follow the troubleshooting decision tree. Identify the problem (15 minutes — faster because the decision tree eliminates guesswork). Complete the repair with pre-positioned materials (30 minutes). Descend (30 minutes). Total: approximately 2 hours.

We cut MTTR by more than 40% on average. For a team operating at 586 feet in a marine environment with limited weather windows, that reduction was the difference between completing two interventions per weather window and completing three. Over six mobilizations, the cumulative schedule savings were measured in days.

## Pre-Positioning Spares When OEM Support Is Unavailable

Aerospace infrastructure has a unique logistics challenge: the equipment is specialized, the OEM support is limited, and the support that does exist is typically available only during business hours Monday through Friday.

When an obstruction lighting fixture fails at 11 PM on a Saturday, the OEM is not answering the phone. When a communications antenna amplifier fails during a pre-launch window, the manufacturer's "expedited" shipping still takes three to five business days. When a control system power supply faults during a mobilization, the nearest distributor with that specific unit in stock is in Orlando — an hour away, assuming they are open.

**The pre-positioning strategy:** Before each mobilization, I developed a failure mode analysis for every critical system we would be working on or near. For each failure mode, I identified the replacement component, the source, and the lead time. Any component with a lead time greater than 4 hours was pre-positioned on site before the mobilization began.

The pre-positioning inventory for a typical mobilization included:

- Obstruction lighting fixtures (complete assemblies, not just lamps)
- LED drivers for each fixture type on the tower
- Photocell assemblies
- Marine-rated wire connectors, terminals, and splice kits
- Conduit fittings and sealing materials
- Antenna amplifiers and associated RF cabling
- Control system power supplies and I/O modules
- Spare rope, carabiners, and pulleys for the rescue equipment (inspected and certified)
- Batteries for all cordless tools, fully charged, plus backup charger

We maintained this inventory in a dedicated, climate-controlled storage locker at the base of the tower. Every item was cataloged — part number, quantity, location in the locker, and the system it supported. The inventory was verified before every mobilization and replenished after every use.

**The economics:** The total pre-positioned inventory cost approximately $15,000 per mobilization. The cost of a failed mobilization — a team of four people at elevation who cannot complete their scope because a $200 part is not on site — was approximately $8,000 per day in direct labor costs alone, not counting the schedule impact of re-mobilizing for a subsequent weather window. One avoided failed mobilization paid for the entire pre-positioning inventory. We avoided at least three over the course of the program, based on components we used from the pre-positioned stock that would not have been available otherwise.

## Six Mobilizations, Zero Incidents

Let me be clear about what zero incidents means in this context, because the term is sometimes used loosely in the industry.

**Zero recordable injuries.** No OSHA recordable events of any kind. No medical treatment beyond first aid (and no first aid events either). No restricted duty. No lost time.

**Zero near misses.** No events that, under slightly different circumstances, could have resulted in injury or property damage. No dropped tools. No slipped equipment. No unexpected wind events that caught the team off guard.

**Zero property damage.** No damage to the tower structure, the installed equipment, or our equipment during any mobilization.

**Zero rescue activations.** The rescue plan was never activated. All team members returned to ground level under their own power after every mobilization.

That record across six separate mobilizations to a 586-foot elevation in a marine environment is not a statistical anomaly. It is the result of specific, deliberate practices.

**Pre-mobilization readiness checks.** Forty-eight hours before each mobilization, the entire team met for a readiness review. We reviewed the work plan, the weather forecast, the rescue plan, the equipment inspection results, the pre-positioned spare inventory, and the communication plan. Any deficiency — a questionable weather window, an equipment item approaching its inspection expiration, a team member who was not feeling 100% — was grounds for postponing the mobilization. We postponed once due to weather uncertainty. That was not a schedule slip. That was risk management working as designed.

**Tool tethering and equipment management.** Every tool carried above 6 feet was tethered to the worker or to an anchor point. No exceptions. A 2-pound wrench dropped from 586 feet reaches terminal velocity and impacts with enough force to penetrate a hard hat. Tool tethering is not a convenience — it is a life-safety control. We used retractable tool lanyards for hand tools and hard-mounted tool holsters for frequently accessed items. Loose hardware (bolts, washers, connectors) was kept in zip-closure pouches clipped to the worker's harness. Nothing loose. Nothing unsecured. Ever.

**Fatigue management.** High-angle work is physically and mentally demanding. The climbing itself is an exertion. The constant vigilance required at elevation — checking tie-off points, monitoring weather, maintaining three points of contact — creates cognitive fatigue that degrades performance over time. We enforced mandatory rest breaks every 90 minutes and a hard maximum of 6 hours at elevation per person per day. No overtime at height. No "let me just finish this one thing." When the clock hits the limit, you come down.

**Team selection and culture.** Not every skilled electrician is suited for high-angle work. Technical competence at ground level does not translate to competence at elevation. The psychological component — the ability to remain calm, focused, and methodical when exposed to height — is not trainable for everyone. I selected team members based on demonstrated comfort at height, rescue certification, physical fitness, and attitude. The team was small — typically four to six people per mobilization — and every member had worked together previously. There were no strangers on the rope.

## Building and Leading a Diverse Cross-Functional Team

The Blue Origin LC-36 team was not a standard electrical crew. The scope required electrical, controls, communications, rigging, and technical climbing competencies. No single trade provided all of those skills. Building the team meant crossing trade boundaries and assembling a group where every member brought a primary skill and at least one secondary skill.

**The team composition:**

- Electricians with climbing certification and marine environment experience
- Controls technicians with RF/antenna system knowledge
- A rigging specialist who also held high-angle rescue instructor certification
- An operations coordinator with logistics and material management experience

Every member of the team was cross-trained in at least one discipline outside their primary trade. The electricians learned basic RF troubleshooting. The controls technician learned electrical circuit diagnostics. The rigging specialist learned enough electrical theory to assist with conduit installation. This cross-training was not about creating generalists — it was about ensuring that at 586 feet, nobody had to wait for a specific person to arrive before work could continue.

**Diversity of background and perspective.** The team included members with military backgrounds, commercial construction experience, industrial maintenance experience, and specialized access (rope access) experience. That diversity was an asset. The military-trained members brought procedural discipline and comfort with operating in high-consequence environments. The commercial construction members brought efficiency and practical problem-solving. The rope access specialists brought technical climbing expertise that the rest of the team learned from.

The culture I built was simple: everyone teaches, everyone learns, everyone goes home. The rope access specialist who could climb the tower in 20 minutes was not more valuable than the operations coordinator who ensured the right parts were staged at the base. Every role was essential. Every person was respected for what they brought. Zero incidents across six mobilizations was a team achievement, not an individual one.

## Working AT Height vs. Working WITH Height

I want to close with the distinction I opened with, because it captures the philosophy behind every practice in this article.

**Working AT height** is reactive. You arrive at the elevation. You deal with whatever you find. You endure the conditions. You manage the fear. You get the job done despite the environment. This is how most high-angle work is approached in the construction industry — as an occupational hazard to be managed, not an operational parameter to be engineered.

**Working WITH height** is proactive. You engineer the access before you climb. You study the structure, the weather patterns, the corrosion rates, the failure modes. You build troubleshooting companions that eliminate return trips. You pre-position spares that eliminate waiting for OEM support. You train every person on the team to perform a rescue so that the rescue plan is not theoretical. You manage fatigue so that the team is sharp at hour five, not just at hour one. You select the team based on demonstrated capability, not just availability.

Working WITH height means the elevation is a known, planned, managed parameter — like voltage, or temperature, or pressure. You do not fear it. You respect it. You design for it. And when the work is complete, everyone descends under their own power, with the job done right and the record clean.

586 feet. Six mobilizations. Zero incidents.

That is what working WITH height looks like.
